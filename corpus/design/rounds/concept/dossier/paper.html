I

The mechanism is established.

A shell is built almost entirely in the dark. Calcification begins five to six hours after ovulation and runs through the night: roughly two grams of calcium in ten hours, on the order of a hundred times the calcium circulating in the hen’s blood at any moment.1 Her gut is emptiest exactly then. Whatever the morning ration carried has long since passed, and what the night demands she draws from medullary bone — the skeleton’s overdraft account.2 A young flock repays the overdraft by noon. Past sixty weeks it stops balancing: shells thin, checks multiply, keels weaken, and the grading belt prices the deficit egg by egg.

Particle size is a clock. Fine limestone dissolves within hours of the morning feed; coarse particles of two to four millimeters lodge in the gizzard and meter calcium out for eight to twelve hours as they grind down.3, 4 Moved to the day’s last ration, the coarse fraction is still paying out at three in the morning, when deposition peaks — supply overlapping demand instead of preceding it. Total daily calcium is unchanged. The diet is unchanged. The candidate is, in its entirety, a change of when.

The effect is measured, not argued from mechanism alone. Across eleven controlled trials and 214,000 hen-weeks, afternoon-weighted coarse calcium raised breaking strength four to six percent and cut late-lay shell defects by 0.8 to 1.7 percentage points; the platform’s pooled model — prespecified, bias-probed, and sealed before this dossier was drafted — puts the blended annual effect for a flock like Site C-4’s at −1.3 points.5 Figure 1 shows the timing argument on the house clock.

IV

What cuts against, answered at full strength.

A brief that hides its weak points is an advertisement. These are Nightshell’s three, stated at the strength their authors gave them.

Finding 1 · Generalization

Four of Exhibit B’s eleven trials ran on brown-egg aviary flocks; Site C-4 is white-egg and cage-free, and the Adversary declines to assume the effect transfers at full size.

AnswerThe dossier does not ask anyone to assume it. The pilot’s first sixty days are the test the record lacks: houses 3 and 7, same grader, same crew, prespecified threshold of −0.8 points. Reproduce it and the question closes; miss it and the pilot self-terminates, this dossier reopening with the field data attached. The gate is written into the relief, not promised after it.

Finding 2 · The tail

Simulation Sim-247 prices a loss in 5.4 percent of futures; its fifth percentile is −$11,200, arriving when an egg-price trough and a limestone-freight spike coincide.

AnswerThe tail is real, bounded, and reversible. Worst-year exposure is the un-amortized retrofit less Exhibit D’s 66-percent buy-back — $62,560 — against a computed net of $170,817, and reversion to single-ration feeding takes one afternoon that no bird will notice. A candidate whose failure mode is “sell back the augers” is as close to option-priced as this pipeline produces.

Finding 3 · The social floor

The tensor’s weakest score is social, 66: the coarse run moves one worker task past 15:00, and no consumer will ever see the difference on a shelf.

AnswerThe crew chose the 15:00 variant themselves from three offered schedules, consent memo at Ex. D at 9, and the moved task rides along on an existing round. Invisibility to consumers is an absence of marketing, not a harm. Sixty-six is a price the tensor has already charged against the aggregate — a price is not a bar, and treating prices as bars is how pipelines learn to lie about them.
